# Procedure: Acoustic Validation and Energy Accounting

## Purpose

Determine whether a candidate device produces the claimed acoustic effect and identify the physical mechanism responsible for the observed behavior.

## 1. Define the claim before testing

Write the claim as a measurable prediction.

Examples of distinct claims:

- pressure decreases at a particular receiver;
- reflected power decreases;
- transmitted power decreases;
- incident acoustic energy is absorbed;
- structural vibration decreases;
- energy is converted into heat;
- a target frequency is attenuated over a defined bandwidth.

These claims are not interchangeable.

Define the metric, baseline, frequency range, source conditions and minimum meaningful effect.

## 2. Establish a baseline

Record the reference system without the candidate device or under another clearly justified control condition.

Keep the source, operating state, receiver positions, room configuration and acquisition settings consistent.

Where installation changes the field, document the installation rather than treating the device as an isolated object.

## 3. Choose the appropriate measurement method

Select instrumentation and procedures appropriate to the claim.

Potential measurements include:

- calibrated sound-pressure measurements;
- frequency-response measurements;
- phase-sensitive measurements;
- sound intensity or power measurements;
- structural vibration measurements;
- temperature measurements;
- decay or ring-down measurements.

Use relevant standards and suitable calibration procedures where applicable.

Do not infer total acoustic power from a single microphone measurement unless the assumptions justify that inference.

## 4. Test reflection, transmission and absorption separately

Where the setup permits, quantify the incident, reflected, transmitted and dissipated power.

For a suitably defined passive system, account for all relevant outgoing propagating modes and any other energy pathways.

Do not assume that the difference between two local sound-pressure levels equals the absorbed power.

If only insertion loss or receiver-level reduction can be measured, report that result without relabeling it as an absorption coefficient.

## 5. Test the proposed internal mechanism

If the hypothesis involves confinement, internal reflections, phase cancellation, resonance or beats, identify a measurable signature that distinguishes the mechanism from alternatives.

Possible signatures include:

- spatial field distributions;
- frequency-dependent reflection and transmission;
- modal frequencies and mode shapes;
- phase relationships between selected measurement locations;
- decay characteristics;
- changes under controlled geometric perturbations;
- changes under controlled damping or boundary conditions.

Select measurements based on the actual formulation. Do not require every method for every device.

## 6. Verify the heat-generation claim

If the hypothesis predicts significant heating:

1. Define the region in which heating is expected.
2. Estimate the incident acoustic power available to the device.
3. Establish the measurement's thermal sensitivity and time scale.
4. Measure temperature change with appropriate controls.
5. account for environmental heat exchange, structural conduction and other heat sources.
6. Compare the inferred thermal energy with the acoustic energy budget.
7. Repeat under controlled operating conditions.

A measured temperature rise alone does not establish that acoustic beats caused it.

If the available acoustic power is insufficient to explain the measured thermal effect, investigate alternative energy sources or measurement errors.

## 7. Use controls and repeatability

Where practical, compare:

- a baseline without the device;
- the candidate device;
- a geometrically similar control that lacks the proposed key feature;
- a modified candidate that selectively changes the hypothesized mechanism.

Repeat measurements and document variation.

Choose controls that discriminate between plausible explanations, rather than merely reproducing the preferred result.

## 8. Compare predictions with data

Use consistent frequency axes, normalization, operating conditions and uncertainty estimates.

Report:

- measured values;
- model predictions;
- uncertainty intervals;
- residuals or discrepancies;
- repeatability;
- deviations from assumptions.

Do not conceal failed predictions by changing the model after each measurement without recording the change.

## 9. Apply a decision rule

Classify the claim as:

- supported within the tested conditions;
- partially supported;
- inconsistent with the measurements;
- inconclusive;
- not yet tested.

State the evidence and limitations for each conclusion.

A mechanism may be physically real but too weak to be useful. Conversely, a device may achieve a useful acoustic result even if the initially proposed explanation is incorrect. Keep mechanism validation and performance validation separate.

## Required deliverables

- Testable claim.
- Baseline and controls.
- Measurement method and calibration information.
- Energy-accounting method where applicable.
- Results and uncertainty.
- Comparison with model predictions.
- Conclusion limited to the tested conditions.
- Next experiment that would most reduce uncertainty.
